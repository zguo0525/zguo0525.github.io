Nobody downloads a model.

They download something they hope will make their day a little better. Then they decide, quickly and without much mercy, whether it did.

Within three weeks of launch, Muse was the top free app in the US App Store and had passed [five million downloads](https://9to5mac.com/2026/09/30/report-metas-muse-crosses-5-million-downloads-amid-massive-advertising-push/), faster than ChatGPT did.

Distribution explains a lot of that. Meta can put an app in front of billions of people. But distribution buys installs. It doesn't buy attachment. As one early reviewer [put it](https://metedata.substack.com/p/what-meta-got-right-with-muse), "People love their muses."

You don't love a feature. You love something that feels like it was made for you.

---

## Start from the person, not the model

Most AI products start from a capability. Here's what the model can do; now go find someone who needs it.

Muse starts from the other end: a person with things to do. You don't prompt it to show off. You tell it what you need, and it gets to work.

The question isn't *what can this model do?* It's *what does this person want?*

---

## Personality is the interface

Most AI products look like tools. Clean. Neutral. A little cold.

Muse took the other side. A face. A name. A personality you shape yourself.

That was a real bet, and not everyone likes it. Some people find it too cute. Fine. A product nobody dislikes is usually a product nobody loves.

Personality isn't decoration. It's how the product feels to use, and how it feels is most of what people remember.

---

## A face that listens

Next comes [Muse Realtime Avatar](https://research.meta.ai/blog/bringing-your-muse-to-life), coming soon. You'll be able to video chat with your Muse.

Why give an AI a face at all?

Because people talk differently when someone is visibly listening. In one classic study, storytellers told their stories [noticeably worse](https://www.responsebasedpractice.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Listeners-as-Co-Narrators.pdf) when the listener stopped reacting. A face that reacts doesn't just look nice. It changes how you talk. It makes you feel *heard*.

So we built it from the user's side, not the model's:

- **Your character stays itself.** You pick who you talk to. A photo moves with restraint, a cartoon moves big, and it's the same character from one turn to the next.
- **Latency is measured from your side.** From the moment you stop talking to the moment it answers. Not how long the model takes.
- **We judged it the way you'd use it.** Real two-to-three-minute conversations, against other products' own live-call experiences. One question: which one would you rather keep talking to?
- **Making it fast had to be invisible.** We cut its compute sharply. The bar was that people couldn't tell.

None of these are model metrics. All of them are things a person feels.

---

## Honest about the gap

User-first also means being honest about how far there is to go.

People in conversation answer each other in about [200 milliseconds](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731/full). Our avatar answers in under a second. That's fast for an avatar. It isn't yet how people talk.

And an agent that knows your life has to earn that. For a personal agent, trust isn't a policy page. It's part of the experience.

---

Downloads are a first impression. The real test is whether Muse becomes a habit: whether people still talk to it in six months because it makes their days better.

The way to pass that test is the same thing that got Muse here. Keep asking what the person on the other side feels. Train on that, not on what makes the model look strongest.

The best AI isn't the one with the highest score. It's the one that makes you feel *seen*.
